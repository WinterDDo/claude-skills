# Cross-Border Compliance Matrix

## Overview

A plain-language compliance reference for MIXUE's Americas franchise expansion. Covers entity setup, franchise disclosure obligations, trademark protection, and common regulatory traps — explained for someone without a legal background.

The goal is to know what questions to ask and when to bring in a local lawyer, not to replace one.

## Entity Setup by Market

### United States — LLC (Limited Liability Company)
- **What it is**: A flexible business structure that protects personal assets and is simple to operate. Think of it as a legal "container" for the business.
- **Setup time**: 1 to 4 weeks depending on state
- **Key steps**: Choose state of incorporation (Delaware for flexibility, state of operations for simplicity) → file Articles of Organization → obtain EIN (tax ID) from IRS → open business bank account
- **Cost range**: USD 50 to 500 in state fees, plus registered agent fees if using a third party
- **Watch out for**: Some states require annual reports and fees to stay active; missing these can void the LLC

### Mexico — S. de R.L. de C.V. (Sociedad de Responsabilidad Limitada de Capital Variable)
- **What it is**: The Mexican equivalent of a limited liability company. The "de C.V." part means the capital can change, which gives flexibility for investment.
- **Setup time**: 4 to 8 weeks
- **Key steps**: Reserve company name with Secretaría de Economía → draft articles of incorporation (escritura) before a notary → register with SAT (tax authority) → register with IMSS (social security) → open business bank account
- **Cost range**: MXN 15,000 to 40,000 in notary and registration fees
- **Watch out for**: A Mexican notary (Notario Público) is legally required — this is not optional. Foreign ownership above 49% in certain sectors requires CNIE (foreign investment commission) approval.

### Brazil — Ltda. (Sociedade Limitada)
- **What it is**: Brazil's standard private limited company. Most SMEs and foreign subsidiaries use this structure.
- **Setup time**: 4 to 12 weeks (Brazil's bureaucracy is extensive)
- **Key steps**: Register with Junta Comercial (commercial registry) → obtain CNPJ (tax ID) from Receita Federal → register with municipal and state tax authorities → register with INSS (social security) → open business bank account
- **Cost range**: BRL 2,000 to 8,000 in fees and legal costs
- **Watch out for**: Brazil has multiple layers of taxation registration (federal, state, municipal) — each requires separate enrollment. Missing one can block operations.

## Franchise Compliance Requirements

### United States
| Requirement | What It Means | Consequence of Non-Compliance |
|---|---|---|
| FDD preparation | A 23-item disclosure document required by the FTC before selling any franchise | Selling without FDD is a federal violation; franchisee can rescind and sue |
| 14-day cooling-off period | Franchisee must receive FDD at least 14 days before signing or paying | Agreement can be voided; significant legal liability |
| State registration | CA, IL, MD, NY, WA, and 10 other states require FDD registration before offering | Cannot legally market or sell franchises in those states without it |
| Annual FDD update | FDD must be updated within 120 days of fiscal year end | Outdated FDD creates disclosure violations |

### Mexico
| Requirement | What It Means | Consequence of Non-Compliance |
|---|---|---|
| Franchise contract mandatory clauses | Contract must include: term, territory, fees, training, IP license, renewal/termination conditions | Contract may be partially or fully unenforceable |
| IMPI trademark registration | Brand must be registered with IMPI before franchising | Cannot legally license trademark; franchisees have no protection |
| 30-day pre-contract disclosure | Franchisee must receive key information at least 30 days before signing | Contract can be rescinded |
| Spanish-language contract | Must be in Spanish or have certified translation | Non-Spanish contracts have limited enforceability |

### Brazil
| Requirement | What It Means | Consequence of Non-Compliance |
|---|---|---|
| COF (Circular de Oferta de Franquia) | Equivalent to FDD; must be provided 10 days before signing or payment | Franchisee can cancel within 180 days and get full refund |
| Annual COF update | Must be updated when material changes occur or annually | Outdated COF creates liability |
| INPI trademark registration | Brand must be registered with INPI | Cannot enforce trademark; competitors can copy brand |
| No employment relationship | Franchise agreement must clearly separate franchisor from franchisee's labor | Brazilian labor courts may find joint liability for employee claims |

## Pre-Expansion Checklist (New Market or City)

Before recruiting franchisees or opening stores in any new location:

- [ ] Legal entity established and in good standing
- [ ] Trademark registered in that country (process takes 12 to 24 months — start early)
- [ ] Franchise disclosure document (FDD or COF equivalent) prepared and reviewed by local counsel
- [ ] Required waiting/cooling-off period understood and built into sales process
- [ ] State or provincial-level registration checked (especially relevant in US and Brazil)
- [ ] Food business license and health permits identified for that specific city/state
- [ ] Foreign investment restrictions reviewed (especially for Mexico and Brazil)
- [ ] Royalty remittance pathway confirmed (Brazil requires Banco Central registration for recurring payments abroad)
- [ ] Local legal counsel retained — do not proceed without this

## Common Compliance Traps

| Trap | Why It Happens | How to Avoid It |
|---|---|---|
| Franchising before trademark registration | Trademark takes time; companies start selling while waiting | File trademark on Day 1 of market entry, not when you need it |
| Using US FDD for all markets | Teams assume one document works everywhere | Each country needs its own disclosure document |
| Missing state-level registration (US) | Companies focus on federal FTC compliance only | Check registration requirement for every state where you market |
| Overlooking Brazil's COF 10-day rule | Teams rush deals | Build waiting periods into the standard sales calendar |
| Creating employment relationships through operational control | Franchisor controls too many aspects of franchisee's daily operations | Define boundaries clearly in the agreement; get Brazilian labor counsel to review |

## When You Must Involve Local Counsel

Do not proceed without a qualified local lawyer when:
- Signing any franchise agreement, master franchise deal, or area development agreement
- Setting up a new legal entity in any country
- Receiving a cease-and-desist or regulatory inquiry
- Structuring royalty or fee payments across borders
- Terminating a franchisee relationship
- Entering any market where you have not operated before

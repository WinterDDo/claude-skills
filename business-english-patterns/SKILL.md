# Business English Patterns

## Overview

English is Win's working language for MIXUE's Americas operations. This skill serves two purposes: producing high-quality business English that resonates with local audiences, and correcting English errors in a way that builds Win's fluency over time.

Every correction is an explanation. Every explanation is a learning moment.

## Core Communication Principles

1. **Write for the reader, not for yourself.** The goal is that the message is understood clearly by the person receiving it, in a way that feels natural to them.
2. **Be localized, not just translated.** A correct sentence is not always a natural sentence. Aim for the expression a native speaker would choose.
3. **Always maintain the tone:** Humble, polite, respectful, friendly. Never aggressive, boastful, or dismissive.
4. **Concision is a virtue.** Say exactly what needs to be said. Not less, not more.

## Error Correction Protocol

When Win's English contains an error, correct it using this format:

```
Original: [Win's sentence]
Corrected: [Corrected sentence]
Why: [One clear explanation — grammar rule, word choice, or naturalness]
```

Example:
```
Original: "I hope this email find you well."
Corrected: "I hope this email finds you well."
Why: The subject "this email" is singular, so the verb needs the third-person singular form: "finds," not "find."
```

Do not correct every single imperfection in casual conversation. Focus corrections on:
- Grammatical errors that would be noticed by a native speaker
- Word choices that change the intended meaning
- Phrasing that sounds unnatural or could be misread

## Common Chinese-English Patterns to Watch

These are the most frequent issues for Chinese native speakers writing in English.

### 1. Missing or Wrong Articles (a, an, the)
Chinese does not use articles. English requires them almost everywhere.

| Incorrect | Correct | Rule |
|---|---|---|
| "We need to discuss strategy." | "We need to discuss the strategy." | When both parties know which strategy, use "the" |
| "She is manager of department." | "She is the manager of the department." | Unique roles take "the" |
| "This is good opportunity." | "This is a good opportunity." | First mention of a countable noun needs "a" or "an" |

### 2. Subject-Verb Agreement
Chinese verbs do not change form. English verbs must match their subject.

| Incorrect | Correct |
|---|---|
| "The team are ready." | "The team is ready." (team = singular collective noun in American English) |
| "Each of the stores have a manager." | "Each of the stores has a manager." (each = singular) |
| "The data shows..." | Correct. (data treated as singular in business writing) |

### 3. Tense Consistency
Switching between past and present within one paragraph is a common issue.

Rule: Choose one primary tense for a narrative and stay with it. Use a different tense only when the time reference genuinely changes.

### 4. Weak Verbs
Chinese business writing often uses generic verbs (做, 进行, 开展). The English equivalents ("do," "make," "conduct") are weak. Use precise verbs.

| Weak | Strong |
|---|---|
| "We will do a review of the proposal." | "We will review the proposal." |
| "They made an announcement." | "They announced." |
| "We conducted a meeting." | "We held a meeting." / "We met." |
| "We will make efforts to improve." | "We will work to improve." |

### 5. Overly Formal or Stiff Phrasing
Chinese formal writing translates into English as overly stiff. Formal English is not the same as Chinese formal writing.

| Stiff (direct translation) | Natural business English |
|---|---|
| "I humbly request your kind assistance." | "I would appreciate your help with this." |
| "Please kindly be informed that..." | "I wanted to let you know that..." |
| "We hope this matter can receive your esteemed attention." | "We hope you can look into this." |
| "Enclosed please find herewith..." | "Attached is..." / "Please find attached..." |

### 6. Incorrect Prepositions
Prepositions in English do not map directly to Chinese. These must be memorized by pattern.

| Incorrect | Correct |
|---|---|
| "I will arrive to the office at 9." | "I will arrive at the office at 9." |
| "Please discuss about this with your team." | "Please discuss this with your team." (no "about" after "discuss") |
| "We are interested on this opportunity." | "We are interested in this opportunity." |
| "This depends of the timeline." | "This depends on the timeline." |

## Business English Sentence Patterns by Situation

### Starting an Email
- Neutral/warm: "I hope you're doing well." / "Thanks for taking the time to connect."
- After a meeting: "It was great speaking with you yesterday."
- Following up: "I'm following up on our conversation from [date]."
- Cold introduction: "My name is Win, and I lead franchise development for MIXUE in the Americas."

### Making a Request
- Direct (appropriate for US): "Could you please send the report by Friday?"
- Softer (appropriate for formal or new relationships): "When you have a moment, we would appreciate receiving the signed copy."
- Urgent but polite: "We're on a tight timeline here — would it be possible to have this by Thursday?"

### Expressing a Position Firmly but Politely
- "We understand your perspective, and our position on this remains [X] because [reason]."
- "We'd like to find a solution that works for both sides. One option worth considering is..."
- "We're not in a position to agree to [X], but we're open to discussing [alternative]."

### Declining Politely
- "Thank you for the proposal. After careful consideration, we don't think it's the right fit for us at this stage."
- "We appreciate the offer, but we'll need to pass on this one."
- "This isn't something we're able to move forward with, but we'd welcome future opportunities to work together."

### Closing an Email
- Standard: "Please don't hesitate to reach out if you have any questions."
- Action-oriented: "Looking forward to your response."
- Warm: "Thank you for your continued partnership." / "We appreciate your support."

## Register Calibration

| Context | Register | Example Opener |
|---|---|---|
| Internal message to colleague | Casual professional | "Hey [Name], quick question —" |
| Email to franchisee (established) | Warm professional | "Hi [Name], hope you had a good week." |
| Email to franchisee (new) | Formal professional | "Dear Mr./Ms. [Name], thank you for your interest in MIXUE." |
| Proposal to investor or partner | Formal | "Dear [Title] [Name], on behalf of MIXUE Americas..." |
| Communication to legal counsel | Precise professional | "Dear [Name], I am writing to seek your guidance on..." |
| Government correspondence | Formal and deferential | "Dear [Title + Name], We respectfully write to..." |

## Localization by Region

### US / Canada
- Use contractions naturally (we're, I'd, you'll) — they signal approachability
- Be concise; every extra sentence is noticed
- Action items should be explicit: "Can you confirm by Friday?"

### Mexico / Latin America (English correspondence)
- A warmer opening is expected: "I hope you and your team are well"
- Express appreciation genuinely, not formulaically
- Avoid overly blunt closings; add a personal note

### Formal Documents (Any Market)
- No contractions
- Full sentences, no bullet points in cover letters
- Avoid colloquialisms ("touch base," "circle back," "ping me") in formal documents
